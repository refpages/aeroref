# Accelerated Frames of Reference
A coordinate system used to measure the position and motion of objects is referred to as a “frame of reference”. 
An inertial frame of reference is one where an object at rest or moving at a constant velocity experiences no net external forces (i.e., Newton's first law applies). 
Most aerospace systems, especially during maneuvers, are more conveniently tackled in non-inertial or accelerated frames, however. 
Such frames come into play when a vehicle is experiencing rapid changes in velocity, direction, or altitude. 
For instance, when an aircraft is turning, an observer on the plane experiences centrifugal forces that do not exist in the (quasi) inertial frame of a stationary observer on the surface of the Earth. 
In these cases, so-called **fictitious** or **frame-based** forces 
such as centrifugal force (due to rotation) and the Coriolis force (due to moving within a rotating reference frame) arise.

To navigate between inertial and accelerated frames, one must apply transformations that account for frame-based forces.

## Kinematics in Accelerated Frames

A time dependent position vector pointing from the origin to a position in the inertial frame A can be defined as

$$
\mathbf{r}_A = \sum_i r_{A,i} \, \hat{\mathbf{e}}_i,
$$
where $A = (\mathbf{\hat{e}}_i, \mathbf{\hat{e}}_j, \mathbf{\hat{e}}_k)$ is the standard unit basis. For instance, a realization in Cartesian 3D of A could read 

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix}  1 & 0 & 0 \\  0 & 1 & 0 \\  0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The same vector in the accelerated frame B reads

$$
\mathbf{r}_B = \sum_i r_{B,i} \, \hat{\mathbf{u}}_i
$$

We will assume that the new basis vectors $\hat{\mathbf{u}}_i(t)$ are time dependent and can be expressed in terms of the unit basis vectors $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_i$ such that we can construct a change-of-basis matrix $B$. In Cartesian 3D $B$ would read 

$$
B(t) = (\hat{\mathbf{u}}_1, \hat{\mathbf{u}}_2, \hat{\mathbf{u}}_3)(t).
$$

We will further assume that we can express the vector $\mathbf{r}_A$ as

$$
\mathbf{r}_A = B \, (\mathbf{r}_B +   \mathbf{R}_{B}) = B \, \mathbf{r}'_B,
$$

where $\mathbf{R}_{B}$ is the vector connecting the origins of the frames $A$ and $B$. To simplfy notation we shall remember that $B=B(t)$ and assume without loss of generatlity that $\mathbf{R}_{B} = 0$  for the remainder of this article.

<Result title="Accelerated Frame to Inertial Frame">
If both origins are identical, i.e., $\mathbf{R}_{B} = 0$, then
$$
\mathbf{r}_A = B \, \mathbf{r}_B,
$$

The velocity in frame $A$ is

$$
\mathbf{v}_A = \frac{d\mathbf{r}_A}{dt}  = \dot{B} \, \mathbf{r}_B + B \, \mathbf{v}_B,
$$

and accelerations can be found by differentiating once more

$$
\mathbf{a}_A = \ddot{B} \, \mathbf{r}_B + 2 \dot{B} \, \mathbf{v}_B + B \, \mathbf{a}_B.
$$
</Result>
Here we have used the product rule, since both $B$ and $\mathbf{r}_B$ may depend on time.
We see that **accelerations in the inertial frame $A$ are not simply a linear combination of acceleration in the accelerated frame $B$**. Instead, the time dependent basis vectors contribure extra terms if $\dot{B}\neq0$. 

<Result title="Inertial Frame to Accelerated Frame">
In order to understand what **velocity and acceleration vectors in the accelerated frame $B$** look like, we can use the fact that positions transform as

$$
\mathbf{r}_B = B^{-1} \, \mathbf{r}_A
$$

For velocities matrix calculus tells us that

$$
\mathbf{v}_B = B^{-1} \, \mathbf{v}_A - B^{-1} \dot{B} B^{-1} \, \mathbf{r}_A.
$$


Finally, accelerations of our test particle in the accelerated frame $B$ read  
$$
\mathbf{a}_B = B^{-1} \, \mathbf{a}_A - B^{-1} \ddot{B} B^{-1} \, \mathbf{r}_A - 2 B^{-1} \dot{B} B^{-1} \, \mathbf{v}_A + 2 B^{-1} \dot{B} B^{-1} \dot{B} B^{-1} \, \mathbf{r}_A
$$
</Result>

<CalloutContainer slot="cards">
<CalloutCard title="Heads up!">

Note that for $\dot{B} = 0$ velocity and acceleration vectors can be attained through a simple change of basis
$ \mathbf{v}_B = B^{-1} \, \mathbf{v}_A $, and  
$\mathbf{a}_B = B^{-1} \, \mathbf{a}_A$.
</CalloutCard>
</CalloutContainer>

## Rotating Frames and the Transport Theorem
Matrices that describe rotating coordinate systems can be considered a special case of time dependent frame transformations. 
Let $\mathbf{r}_A(t)$ be the position vector in the inertial frame, $\mathbf{r}_B(t)$ be the position vector in the rotating frame
and $B=R(t)$ a rotation matrix from frame B to frame A, then
$$
\mathbf{r}_A(t) = R[\omega(t)] \, \mathbf{r}_B(t).
$$
Realizations of rotation matrices around the x, y and z axis are 
$$
R_x(\omega) = \begin{bmatrix}  1 &  0  & 0 \\ 0 &  \cos(\omega) & -\sin(\omega) \\ 0 &  \sin(\omega) &  \cos(\omega) \end{bmatrix},
$$
$$
R_y(\omega) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\omega) &  0  & \sin(\omega) \\0 & 1 & 0 \\  -\sin(\omega) & 0  & \cos(\omega) \end{bmatrix},
$$
$$
R_z(\omega) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\omega) & -\sin(\omega) & 0 \\ \sin(\omega)  & \cos(\omega) & 0 \\ 0 &       0  &     1 \end{bmatrix},
$$
respectively. Calculating derivatives and inverting the above matrices is possible to calculate velocity and acceleration in $A$ and $B$ frames is possible, but not very convenient.
The **Transport Theorem** provides a more efficient way to calculate vector components in accelerated (rotating) frames from inertial-frame quantities.

<Result title="Transport Theorem">
The Transport Theorem states that time derivatives of a vector $\mathbf{f}$ in inertial frames can be expressed in rotating frame quantities as
$$
\frac{d}{dt} \mathbf{f}_A = B\, \frac{d}{dt}  \mathbf{f}_B + \boldsymbol{\omega}\times (B\, \mathbf{f}_B).
$$
</Result>

How come? The change in time of the unit vectors that make up matrix $B = (\mathbf{\hat{u}}_x,\mathbf{\hat{u}}_y,\mathbf{\hat{u}}_z)$ can be described as
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{\hat{u}}_x = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{\hat{u}}_x,\quad \frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{\hat{u}}_y = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{\hat{u}}_y,\quad \frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{\hat{u}}_z = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{\hat{u}}_z
$$
since the rotation has to happen in the plane orthogonal to $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ and vectors $\mathbf{\hat{u}}_{x,y,z}$. To this we need to add changes of $\mathbf{f}$ in the $B$ frame, and voila. 

Using the Transport Theorem we can derive the well known equations for transforming accelerations and velocities in rotating frames.
Taking the time derivative of $\mathbf{r}_A$ we find
$$
\frac{d\mathbf{r}_A}{dt} = \mathbf{v}_A = \dot{R} \, \mathbf{r}_B + R \, \mathbf{v}_B,
$$
and since (matrix component vector wise)
$$
\dot{R} = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times R,
$$
we have
$$
\mathbf{v}_A = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (R \, \mathbf{r}_B) + R \, \mathbf{v}_B.
$$
Differentiating again we find the well known transformation equation for accelerations:
$$
\mathbf{a}_A = \dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \times (R \, \mathbf{r}_B) + 
                \boldsymbol{\omega} \times [\boldsymbol{\omega} \times (R \, \mathbf{r}_B)] + 
                2 \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (R \, \mathbf{v}_B) + R \, \mathbf{a}_B.
$$
This above expression contains the following acceleration terms:  
- Euler: &nbsp; &nbsp;&nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; $\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \times (R \, \mathbf{r}_B)$,    
- Centripetal: &nbsp; $\boldsymbol{\omega} \times [\boldsymbol{\omega} \times (R \, \mathbf{r}_B)]$, 
- Coriolis: &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; $2 \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (R \, \mathbf{v}_B)$, and
- In Frame: &nbsp; &nbsp;&nbsp; $R \, \mathbf{a}_B$.
