# Mathematical Preliminaries

## Overview

This page provides an overview of the basics of vector and tensor theory, touching on the fundamental principles that make up vector and tensor calculus. These ideas will be viewed through the lens of indicial notation, a mathematical notation that significantly simplifies complex expressions involving vectors and tensors. Coordinate transformations will also be explored in this section.

### Vector Theory

This section will include an overview of vector basics including vector algebra, vector and scalar fields, as well as fundamental identities involving vectors.

#### Vector Algebra

A *vector* is a directed line segment, possessing the qualities of both magnitude and direction. The magnitude being the scale of the vector and the direction being the relative orientation of the vector in a given coordinate system.

(Visual representation of a vector)

In mathematical notation, the vector, $\vec{A}$, can be written as follows:

$$
\vec{A} = A_x \vec{e}_x + A_y \vec{e}_y + A_z \vec{e}_z
$$

where $\vec{e_x}$, $\vec{e_y}$, and $\vec{e_z}$ are the unit vectors (magnitude of one) in the $x$, $y$, and $z$ directions, respectively. $A_x$, $A_y$, and $A_z$ are the scalar values corresponding to each of these three unit vectors.
Each of these three terms are called *components* of vector $\vec{A}$. That is, each component describes the portion of $\vec{A}$ that is parallel to the respective axis of said component. Together, all three components make up the resultant vector of $\vec{A}$.

Given the components of $\vec{A}$, the total magnitude can be found:

$$
|A| = \sqrt{(A_x)^2 + (A_y)^2 + (A_z)^2}
$$

Let another vector, $\vec{B}$ be given as:

$$
\vec{B} = B_x \vec{e}_x + B_y \vec{e}_y + B_z \vec{e}_z,
$$

two vectors can be added such that:

$$
\vec{A} + \vec{B} = (A_x + B_x) \hat{\imath} + (A_y + B_y) \hat{\jmath} + (A_z + B_z) \hat{k}.
$$

**Note:** $\hat{\imath}$, $\hat{\jmath}$, and $\hat{k}$ are an alternative and common way to denote the unit vectors $\vec{e_x}$, $\vec{e_y}$, and $\vec{e_z}$, respectively.
Vectors can also be multiplied by means of a scalar, vector, or dyadic product. A scalar product of two vectors, also known as a *dot product*, will yield a scalar value and is given as:

$$
\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = A_x B_x + A_y B_y + A_z B_z
$$

A vector product, also known as a *cross product*, yields a vector that is orthogonal to both original vectors and is given as

$$
\vec{A} \times \vec{B} =
\begin{vmatrix} 
\hat{\imath} & \hat{\jmath} & \hat{k} \\ 
A_x & A_y & A_z \\ 
B_x & B_y & B_z
\end{vmatrix}
= (A_y B_z - A_z B_y) \hat{\imath} 
 - (A_x B_z - A_z B_x) \hat{\jmath} 
 + (A_x B_y - A_y B_x) \hat{k}
$$

The *dyadic product* of two vectors yields a 3x3 matrix, or a *tensor*, and is given as:

$$
\vec{A} \otimes \vec{B} =
\begin{bmatrix}
A_x B_x & A_x B_y & A_x B_z \\
A_y B_x & A_y B_y & A_y B_z \\
A_z B_x & A_z B_y & A_z B_z
\end{bmatrix}
$$

#### Scalar and Vector Fields

A *field* is any quantity that varies spatially. That is, if a value depends on $x, y,$ and $z$.

A *scalar field* is defined by a scalar quantity expressed as a function of cartesian coordinates. An example of a scalar field could be a map showing the temperature at different locations. \\

A *vector field* is formed when the components of a vector depend on the spatial coordinates of the vector. An example of a vector field could be a weather map showing the wind direction at different points in space. \\

Scalar and vector fields have many useful applications, but to be able to explore these applications, the fundamentals of vector calculus must be understood. The del/nabla operator, $\nabla,$ can be applied to both scalars and vectors in several ways in order to obtain useful information about a given value or field.

The *gradient of a scalar*, $f$, yields a vector, defined as follows:

$$
\nabla f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \hat{\imath} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \hat{\jmath} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} \hat{k}
$$

The *divergence of a vector*, $\vec{A}$, is given as the dot product between the vector and the del operator and yields a scalar as follows:

$$
\nabla \cdot \vec{A} = \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial z}
$$

The *curl of a vector*, $\vec{A}$, also yields a vector and is given as the cross product between the vector and the del operator as follows:

$$
    \nabla \times \vec{A} = \begin{vmatrix}
\hat{\imath} & \hat{\jmath} & \hat{k} \\
\frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ 
A_x & A_y & A_z 
\end{vmatrix} = \left( \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial z} \right) \hat{\imath} - \left( \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial z} \right) \hat{\jmath} + \left( \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y} \right) \hat{k}
$$

The *gradient of a vector*, $\vec{A}$, yields a tensor and is given as the dyadic product between the vector and the del operator as follows:

$$
\nabla \otimes \vec{A} = \begin{bmatrix}
\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial x} \\
\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial y} \\
\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial z} & \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial z} & \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial z}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

### Indicial Notation

Indicial notation is a mathematical shorthand that is useful for concisely representing complex operations. Any number of dimensions can be represented through this notation, but for the sake of this course, we will work with the standard three dimensions, representative of the three spatial dimensions.

Using indicial notation a vector, $\vec{A}$, consisting of three components ($A_1$, $A_2$, $A_3$) can be written simply as $A_i$ where $i = 1, 2, 3$. A tensor can be similarly condensed. Given:

$$
B = \begin{bmatrix}
    B_{11} & B_{12} & B_{13} \\
    B_{21} & B_{22} & B_{23} \\
    B_{31} & B_{32} & B_{33}
\end{bmatrix},
$$

the same tensor can be written simply as $B_{ij}$, with $i = 1,2,3$ and $j= 1,2,3$.

(The number of terms represented by a certain notation)

In general, the number of terms represented by an indicial term is equal to the number of dimensions (three for all purposes in this course) raised to the power of the number of \textit{free indices} in the term. Free vs repeated indices will be discussed in more detail shortly.

Indicial notation assists us in consolidating expressions that would normally be quite cumbersome to write out in full. For example, the dot product between two vectors can be written using indicial notation as follows:

$$
\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = A_1 B_1 + A_2 B_2 + A_3 B_3 = \sum_{i=1}^{3} A_i B_i
$$

This expression can be further simplified using Einstein's summation convention. This convention states that (twice) repeated indices are summed over 1, 2, and 3. For example:

$$
A_i B_i = A_1 B_1 + A_2 B_2 + A_3 B_3
$$

$$
D_{ii} = D_{11} + D_{22} + D_{33}
$$

There are two types of indices in this form of notation, *free indices* and *dummy (repeated) indices*.
- A *free index* appears only once per term and ranges from 1 to 3, creating 3 separate equations. The free index must match on both sides of a given equation.
- A *dummy index* appears twice per term and implies summation.
Some examples involving free and dummy indices follow:

Examples: 
- $A_i B_i C_{ii}$ is an invalid expression, as the "dummy index" appears more than twice in this term.

- $A_{ij} X_j = C_i$ is a valid expression with free index $i$ and dummy index $j$, notice that the free index $i$ is the same on both sides of the equation. Expanded, this equation results in 3 distinct terms such that:

$$
C_i =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        C_1 \\
        C_2 \\
        C_3
    \end{bmatrix} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        A_{11}X_1 + A_{12}X_2 + A_{13}X_3 \\
        A_{21}X_1 + A_{22}X_2 + A_{23}X_3 \\
        A_{31}X_1 + A_{32}X_2 + A_{33}X_3
    \end{bmatrix}
$$

- $A_i B_i C_{kl} = D_{kl}$ is a valid expression with two free variables ($k$ and $l$) and one dummy variable ($i$). This expression will result in a 3x3 tensor where $k$ is a given row and $l$ is a given column.


If a *no sum* condition is specified, terms at each index become independent and are not summed with each other:

$$
A_{ii} + B_{ii} = C_{ii} \quad \text{(no sum)} \quad \Rightarrow \quad
\begin{cases}
A_{11} + B_{11} = C_{11} \\
A_{22} + B_{22} = C_{22} \\
A_{33} + B_{33} = C_{33}
\end{cases}
$$

Partial derivatives of a term with respect to $x_i$ are abbreviated with a comma followed by an $i$. For example:

- The *divergence of a vector* is written as:
$$
\nabla \cdot \vec{u} = \frac{\partial u_1}{\partial x_1} + \frac{\partial u_2}{\partial x_2} + \frac{\partial u_3}{\partial x_3} = u_{i,i}
$$

- The *gradient of a vector* is written as:

$$
\large
u_{i,j} = 
\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{\partial u_1}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial u_1}{\partial x_2} & \frac{\partial u_1}{\partial x_3} \\
\frac{\partial u_2}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial u_2}{\partial x_2} & \frac{\partial u_2}{\partial x_3} \\
\frac{\partial u_3}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial u_3}{\partial x_2} & \frac{\partial u_3}{\partial x_3}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

- The *gradient of a two-tensor* is written as:
$$
D_{ij, j} = \frac{\partial D_{ij}}{\partial x_j}
$$

The *kronecker delta*, $\delta$, is a function defined such that

$$
\delta_{ij} =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } i = j \\
0 & \text{if } i \ne j
\end{cases}
$$

Essentially, the kronecker delta defines a tensor that is equivalent to a 3x3 identity matrix. In multiplication, it behaves the same way by changing indices on other terms. For example:

- $A_i \delta_{ij} = A_j$
- $D_{ij} \delta_{jk} = D_{ik}$
- $C_{ijk} \delta_{ij} = C_{jjk} = C_{11k} + C_{22k} + C_{33k}$

The *alternator*, $\epsilon_{ijk}$, is defined such that

(Alternator table)

The alternator becomes useful when computing vector cross products, due to the identity:

$$
(\vec{A} \times \vec{B})_i = \epsilon_{ijk} A_j B_k
$$

Some other useful relations are included below:

- Divergence of a vector: $\nabla \cdot \vec{A} = A_{i,j}$
- Curl of a vector: $(\nabla \times \vec{A})_i = \epsilon_{ijk} A_{k, j}$
- Gradient of a vector: $(\nabla \vec{A})_{ij} = A_{i,j}$
- Gradient of a scalar: $(\nabla T)_i = T_j$
- Laplacian of a scalar: $\nabla^2 \phi = \phi_{,ii}$
- Dyadic product of vectors: $(\vec{a} \otimes \vec{b})_{ij} = a_i b_j$

By use of the identities we have learned, it is possible to prove certain relations by rearranging the terms of a given expression. An example of an indicial proof follows:

$$
\begin{gather*}
\vec{a} \cdot (\vec{b} \times \vec{c}) = a_i (\vec{b} \times \vec{c})_i \\
= a_i \epsilon_{ijk} b_j c_k \\
= (a_i \epsilon_{ijk} b_j)c_k \\
= \epsilon_{ijk} a_i b_j \\
= (\epsilon_{kij} a_i b_j)_k c_k \\
= (\vec{a} \times \vec{b}) \cdot \vec{c} \\
\therefore \vec{a} \cdot (\vec{b} \times \vec{c}) = (\vec{a} \times \vec{b}) \cdot \vec{c}
\end{gather*}
$$

**Note:** $\epsilon_{ijk} = \epsilon_{kij}$ because both are even permutations of the alternator.
